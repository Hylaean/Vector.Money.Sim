An open research program in monetary & ecological economics

Money has one dimension. The planet has nine.

Every ecological fact the economy must act on gets compressed into a single number — a price — before anyone's budget feels it. Vector Money is a testable alternative: currency whose components are tied, one to one, to the biophysical stocks the economy draws down. We don't advocate it. We simulate it, stress it, and try to break it — in public.

§ 1 · The problem

A price is a damage function that clears markets.

Economists spent a decade showing that climate models fail when they compress the Earth system into one scalar damage number. The same critique applies to the instrument we actually live inside: money.

Prices do carry ecological information — eventually. But they carry it compressed and late, and they are latest exactly when speed matters most: when the environment itself is drifting. Six of nine planetary boundaries are already transgressed, and the economy steers all of them with, at best, one thermostat.

Weitzman proved in 1974 that when damages have thresholds, quantity instruments beat price instruments. Fifty years later, every implementation of that insight manages a single resource with at most two levers. The obvious generalization — n quantity instruments, as spendable as cash — has, to our knowledge, never been built, simulated, or tested. That is the gap this project fills.

§ 2 · The proposal

Not one currency reform. A continuum you can experiment on.

A wallet holds cash plus one token dimension per biophysical stock. Purchases cost cash and their resource footprint. Tokens arrive each period as your equal share of each stock's estimated sustainable yield.

One parameter — convertibility, λ — sets how freely cash substitutes for missing tokens. At λ = 1, tokens are just prices: ordinary scalar money. At λ = 0, tokens are strictly binding: pure vector money. Scalar and vector money are endpoints of one dial, which turns a century-old ideological argument into a controlled experiment.

Special cases recover familiar proposals — personal carbon allowances, the “carbon coin,” WWII points rationing (a real national-scale dual currency that worked for four years). Vector money is what they generalize to.

§ 3 · First evidence

Same economy, same shocks. Only the money changes.

Twenty households, four regenerating stocks with ecological boundaries, an issuing institution that only sees lagged data — and a climate that either holds still or drifts into a permanently harsher regime.

Below: the water stock, the one climate shocks hit hardest. Under scalar money, prices react too slowly to drift; the stock is drained to near zero and never recovers. Under vector money, issuance tightens with the estimated yield: the stock is driven down by the same shocks — then comes back above its boundary.

The difference is not degree. It is recovery.

12 seeds × 200 periods, rule-based agents. Welfare = mean need satisfaction (0–1). Overshoot = cumulative depth of boundary violation. Every number reproducible: python -m experiments.run_matrix

Finding one · the honest trade In a stable world, scalar money wins — roughly 60% more welfare, zero overshoot. Under a shifting climate, vector money's cumulative overshoot is roughly half scalar's (29.8 ± 9.6 vs 60.0 ± 18.1, 12 seeds — a directional finding, not a precise one) and inequality of essential consumption falls by ~80%, at a real welfare cost. The case for vector money is exactly as strong as your belief that the world has stopped holding still.
Finding two · the leak Sweeping the convertibility dial: by 25% convertibility the ecological protection is gone entirely (overshoot ≈ 62 — indistinguishable from scalar money; the collapse happens somewhere between 0% and 25%, below our sweep's resolution). Friedman's black-market objection, quantified. Meanwhile in stable worlds, partial convertibility beats both endpoints on welfare (0.91 vs 0.84). The dial's interior is interesting — just not where we expected.
§ 4 · What would make this wrong

The critiques are the research program.

Vector money is architecturally a rationing system, and rationing has famous failure modes. We don't dodge them. Each one is a testable prediction against the thesis, built into the experiments.

Hayek, 1945

The information problem

Issuance needs a central estimator of sustainable yield; prices aggregate dispersed knowledge for free. Our test: the issuer is deliberately dumb — lagged, drift-blind — and we measure exactly what that ignorance costs against the market's.

Friedman & Stigler, 1946

The black market

Rationing plus prices breeds arbitrage. Our test: the λ dial is a legal black market with a measurable premium — and it already produced a result: the protection dies at 25% leakage.

Camera, 2024 · Fawcett, 2010

The complexity backfire

In laboratories, added monetary dimensionality can hurt welfare even when theory approves — people mis-budget across dimensions. Our test: the human arm. If vector money only works for software, that is a decisive, publishable failure.

Thaler, 1999

Mental accounting as bias

Behavioral economics treats non-fungibility as an error to correct. Vector money bets it is an institution to build. Our test: the disagreement is empirical — does enforced earmarking help or hurt when the constraint is physically real?

§ 5 · The method

Four kinds of minds, one game.

An institutional result that holds only for the agents it was tuned on is an artifact. So the identical economy — same observations, same actions, same budget rules — is played by four different substrates.

If a result survives rules, reinforcement learners, language models and people, it is a candidate institutional fact. And the comparison runs both ways: populations of LLM agents are becoming real economic actors, and nobody knows which monetary institutions keep them inside physical limits. Every vector-money experiment is also a cooperative-AI experiment.
